Human ratings first; translated conditions as secondary probes.

Good morning. I am Naruki Shirahama from Shimonoseki City University.

Today I will discuss how six language models score emotions in literary texts.

Our reference is the emotion ratings of Japanese student readers.

We also examine changes in translated English and Chinese conditions.

This is a compact screening study.

It does not identify a universally best model.

ICECCME 2026 · Motivation

Similar outputs are not enough

Comparisons show differences

  • Model versus model
  • Japanese versus translated conditions
  • Emotion scores can change

Human ratings give a reference

  • Which scores are closer to readers?
  • How large is the disagreement?
  • Where do the errors occur?

Stability and agreement with humans answer different questions.

Many studies compare scores across models or languages.

These comparisons show differences, but they do not show which scores agree with human readers.

Similar scores across languages do not prove agreement with humans.

A model may be stable and still disagree with the human reference.

We therefore keep human agreement and translated-condition stability separate.

We use continuous scores because our question concerns emotion intensity, rather than only an emotion label.

ICECCME 2026 · Two evaluation questions

One benchmark, two questions

Primary: alignment with Japanese human ratings

How close are Japanese model scores to Japanese human ratings?

Reference: Japanese human VAS means

Secondary: translated-condition drift

How much do English and Chinese model scores change from Japanese?

Reference: the same model's Japanese scores

Less change across languages does not prove closer agreement with humans.

Our primary question is alignment with Japanese human ratings.

We compare each model's Japanese scores with the human ratings.

English and Chinese conditions are secondary probes of change from the same model's Japanese scores.

Less change does not prove closer agreement with humans.

Neutral-reader model task

Works: T1 · The Pocket Watch; T2 · The Money and the Pistol; T3 · The Tattered Ostrich.

Collection: OpenRouter · 17–19 April 2026 (JST). The six models are listed in Table I.

We use three literary works: The Pocket Watch, The Money and the Pistol, and The Tattered Ostrich.

Readers rated interest, surprise, sadness, and anger on a Visual Analogue Scale from zero to one hundred.

We collected ninety-one responses from Japanese student readers.

Each story-emotion cell had eighty-six to ninety-one usable ratings.

The model panel has six models, three languages, three works, and ten repetitions.

We used one neutral-reader task at temperature zero point four.

This gives five hundred forty runs and two thousand one hundred sixty scores.

Calls were collected from April seventeen to nineteen, twenty twenty-six.

Ninety run rows needed targeted retries.

All rows then had valid scores.

No scores were imputed.

ICECCME 2026 · Metrics

Two distances, two reference profiles

Japanese MAE

Distance between:

Japanese model means
and Japanese human VAS means

Average absolute difference over 12 cells.

Translated-condition drift

Distance between:

English or Chinese model means
and the same model's Japanese means

Average absolute difference over 12 cells.

Model means use 10 repetitions. Average drift = (EN drift + ZH drift) / 2.

Lower values are better on each axis. There is no combined deployment score.

Each model-language condition gives twelve story-emotion means.

We average the ten model repetitions within each cell.

Japanese MAE compares model means with Japanese human means.

MAE means mean absolute error.

We average the absolute score differences across the twelve cells.

Drift compares English or Chinese means with the same model's Japanese means.

Average drift is the mean of English drift and Chinese drift.

Lower values mean closer agreement or less change, respectively.

The two metrics must remain separate.

Pearson and Spearman correlations are only exploratory diagnostics.

ICECCME 2026 · Uncertainty and sensitivity

A point estimate is only the start

Sampling uncertainty

  • 10,000 cluster-bootstrap replicates
  • Resample respondents and logical runs
  • Keep four emotions together per run
  • Percentile 95% intervals

Benchmark composition

  • Omit each of 3 stories
  • Omit each of 4 emotions
  • Recompute the full ordering
  • 7 deterministic leave-one-out checks

The intervals are conditional on this fixed 12-cell benchmark.

The benchmark is small, so a point estimate is not enough.

We used a cluster bootstrap with ten thousand replicates.

We sampled human respondents and model runs separately.

Each model run keeps its four emotion scores together.

The same resampled human profile is used for all models in each replicate.

We report ninety-five percent percentile intervals.

These intervals apply to this fixed set of twelve cells.

They do not describe uncertainty for new literary works.

We also removed each story, and then each emotion, one at a time.

These leave-one-out checks show how the ordering depends on the benchmark composition.

ICECCME 2026 · Limitations

A compact pilot has clear boundaries

Limited coverage

  • 3 works; 4 fixed emotions
  • One student-reader population
  • 6 selected models; one neutral task
  • No matched EN/ZH human reference

Limited interpretation

  • Text + instructions change language
  • Translations not independently validated
  • Translation provenance incomplete
  • Provider routes and aliases unpinned

Conditional intervals do not establish validity for new works, readers, prompts or languages.

This is a pilot with three works, four fixed emotions, one student-reader population, and six selected models.

We used one neutral-reader prompt and fixed translations.

We did not collect matched human ratings for the English or Chinese versions.

The translations were checked by the research team, but not independently validated.

The translation system, version, and date were not recorded.

Text and instruction language change together.

We cannot separate language, translation, and cultural effects.

Provider routes and model aliases were not fixed, so exact API replay may change.

Our intervals describe sampling uncertainty for these twelve cells.

They do not support generalization to new works, reader groups, prompts, or languages.

ICECCME 2026 · Conclusions

Keep the reference and the uncertainty visible

  1. Two axes: human agreement and translated-condition drift.
  2. Three checks: uncertainty, cell errors, composition sensitivity.
  3. Stage-one screening: broader human validation must follow.

No unique top model is resolved within the four lowest Japanese MAE point estimates.

The main lesson is to keep the human reference explicit.

Japanese human alignment and translated-condition drift answer different questions.

A small point-error difference is not enough to identify a winning model.

On this benchmark, the top four are not pairwise resolved.

Every story or emotion omission changes the full order.

Compact screening should report uncertainty, cell-level errors, and composition sensitivity alongside point estimates.

This is a stage-one screening protocol, not a deployment certificate.

The next step requires more works, broader reader populations, additional prompts, and matched human references in the translated conditions.
